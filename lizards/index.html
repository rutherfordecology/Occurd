Lizards Near Me v1.0

What it does

It asks iNaturalist which lizards have been recorded near you in the last 20 years and sorts them by how often they turn up, so the top of the list is your best bet. Choose in daylight and you get the baskers — skinks and green geckos — plus the night geckos you can realistically turn up under a rock. Choose after dark and you get the geckos that only come out once the torch is needed.

Geckos, skinks and tuatara. Marine turtles and sea snakes are filtered out; they come under the same branch of the tree but turn up only as beach-cast records.

Why the search area starts at 50 km

This is the important thing to understand about lizard records in New Zealand. More than half of them have a deliberately falsified location. The Department of Conservation and iNaturalist obscure threatened species, and green geckos in particular are poached for the overseas pet trade, so their published position is shifted randomly by up to about 25 kilometres.

Of 16,000 research-grade reptile records in New Zealand, around 8,600 are obscured this way. Near Wellington, 18 of the 20 species you would be shown are there because of an obscured record. Searching a tight radius would therefore be theatre: it would look precise and be wrong. Starting at 50 km means the displacement is always smaller than the search, so the records land inside it honestly. Anything obscured is flagged on its card.

So read this as lizards living around here, not lizards at your feet. That is genuinely all the data can support, and it is the right answer anyway — these are protected animals, and several are poaching targets.

Day or night

New Zealand's herpetofauna divides along genus lines almost perfectly, so this is a short hand-checked table rather than a guess: skinks and green geckos by day, every other gecko by night, with tuatara emerging at dusk.

Nocturnal geckos still appear in the daytime list, because turning rocks and lifting bark is how most of them are actually found. That distinction is why the timing is not simply read off the observation timestamps: tested against five species, nocturnal geckos returned only 17–22% night-time observations against 4–13% for diurnal ones. The timestamp records how people survey, not when the animal is awake.

Photos

Photographs come from iNaturalist and are shown only where the photographer has applied a Creative Commons licence. Each one is credited, and tapping any photo opens it large.

Finding a usable one takes three goes. A species' headline photo is often all rights reserved, so the app then checks the taxon's photo gallery, and if that is also restricted — or holds a single image, as happens more than you would think — it searches the observations themselves filtered by licence. A blank tile means all three came up empty, which is now rare.

Your location

Your position never leaves your device un-rounded — it is snapped to roughly 100 m before any search is sent, which makes no difference across a 50 km radius. It is not stored, and it is never sent to the analytics below: it does not appear in the address bar, so nothing can pick it up from there either.

Analytics

This page uses Google Analytics, to see how many people find it and which parts get used. It records which screens are opened and whether a search was for daylight or after dark, along with the search radius and which options were ticked. Google also derives a rough country or city from your IP address, as it does on any site using it, and sets cookies to recognise a returning browser.

Nothing that identifies you personally is sent, and neither your coordinates nor the species you looked at are included. A tracker blocker will stop all of it without affecting the app.
